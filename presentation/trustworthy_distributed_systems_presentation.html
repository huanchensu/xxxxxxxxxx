Section 1
What is a Distributed System?
Core Definition

A system that connects multiple independent computers (nodes) via a network, yet appears to the end-user as a single, unified entity.

Core Engineering Philosophy: Design for Failure

Hardware damage and network disconnections are not accidental glitches—they are statistical inevitabilities.

The system assumes components will fail at any moment, ensuring uninterrupted service through isolation and automatic recovery.

"Leveraging the eternal laws of mathematics and logic to compensate for the transient reliability of physical hardware."

Chaos Engineering / Chaos Monkey

A pioneer open-source tool developed by Netflix that randomly terminates production server instances to force and validate system fault tolerance.

Section 2: Trust Issues
The Business Trust Chasm
Case Background:

Gogoro (managing energy & point reward systems) and Taishin Bank (managing financial & co-branded credit card systems) each possess robust, highly sophisticated distributed systems.

The Soul Question: Do these two separate systems inherently trust each other? The answer is NO.

Technical Reality (Zero Trust)

Cross-organizational system designs must fundamentally be built on the premise of absolute mutual distrust. Direct integration exposes both parties to critical risks:

1. Malicious Tampering

Historical records could be maliciously altered by internal personnel from either side.

2. Transaction Drops

Network fluctuations during data transmission can lead to dropped packets or missing logs.

3. System Bugs

A logical error in one party's software directly corrupts and mismatches the other party's financial books.

Fatal Flaw (Synchronous Blocking): If one party suddenly disconnects during the second phase, the other party is completely "locked" in a waiting state. The single point of failure propagates, making real-time cross-organizational connections unviable.

The Compromise in Business: Post-Event Reconciliation

Since real-time trust cannot be achieved technically, enterprises resort to exporting separate CSV files at the end of each business day (T+1) to perform manual or automated batch matching.

  • Extremely poor timeliness
  • High labor costs for error-catching
  • Endless account balancing headaches

"Reconciliation" is the expensive tax enterprises pay for a systemic lack of external technical trust.

Section 3: The Essence
CAP & PACELC Theorems
The CAP Theorem

Consistency (C), Availability (A), and Partition Tolerance (P) cannot be simultaneously achieved; you can only choose two. Since real-world networks always face partitions (P), engineers must make a hard choice between CP and AP.

The Advanced Extension: PACELC Theorem

If P (In the presence of a network partition): Do you choose Availability or Consistency?

Else (When the network operates normally): Do you choose Latency or Consistency?

An Intuitive Analogy: Taipei & New York Luxury Boutiques

During Disconnection (If P):

Do you risk overselling items to keep the store open (A), or do you halt transactions to preserve accurate data (C)?

During Normal Operations (Else):

Do you allow instant checkout and match records asynchronously later (L), or do you make customers wait for global, synchronized confirmation (C)?

Section 4
Risks of Shared Systems

Beyond the Corporate Firewall: To permanently eliminate reconciliation, organizations require a shared ledger. However, entering an open network maintained by multiple entities exposes the data to two fatal malicious threats:

Threat One: Sybil Attack (女巫攻擊)

A malicious actor can spin up thousands of "virtual fake nodes" at virtually zero cost.

If the system relies on a naive "one node, one vote" consensus mechanism, the attacker instantly secures a majority vote, gaining full power to hijack and alter the ledger.

Threat Two: Byzantine Generals Problem

Traditional distributed systems only guard against node crashes (fail-stop faults).

A shared ledger must guard against malicious nodes that actively lie, broadcast false data, send contradictory instructions, or attempt to overwrite past transaction history.

Section 5: Solutions
Bitcoin's Engine: Transaction Survival
Phase One: Transaction Generation & Broadcast

When a user triggers a cross-organizational action (e.g., swapping a Gogoro battery, which must simultaneously trigger a Taishin co-branded card point deduction), the system:

  1. Generates a transaction block
  2. Signs it with a cryptographic private key
  3. Broadcasts it into the peer-to-peer (P2P) network's Memory Pool (Mempool)
Phase Two: Ruthless Independent Verification

Every independent node that receives the transaction subjects it to cold, objective mathematical and cryptographic validation:

  • Is the digital signature entirely valid and authentic?
  • Does the originating account possess a sufficient balance or reward points?
The Line of Byzantine Defense:

If a rogue party attempts to forge a fake transaction, the cryptographic verification fails instantly. Honest nodes discard it immediately, preventing it from ever entering the pool.

Section 5: Solutions
The Computational Moat (PoW)
Phase Three: Packaging Transactions

Nodes (miners) aggregate valid corporate transactions from the Mempool into a candidate block. This block header contains a critical variable: a Nonce (Number used once).

Phase Four: Finding the Golden Nonce

The network protocol dictates that nodes must blindly guess the value of the Nonce, plugging it into a double SHA-256 cryptographic hash formula until the resulting output is less than a dynamically adjusted difficulty target.

SHA-256(SHA-256(Block Header)) < Target
Brute-Force Computing Without Shortcuts

There are no mathematical shortcuts to this formula. Nodes must rely on raw computing power to execute a brute-force search (hash collisions), consuming real-world physical computational energy and time.

Trust is anchored directly in physical energy expenditure.

Section 5: Solutions
Thermodynamics vs. Sybil Attacks

The Paradigm Shift: From "1 IP = 1 Vote" to "1 Hashrate = 1 Vote"

The Attacker's Script (Virtual World)

A malicious attacker can write a script to instantiate 1,000,000 virtual, fake IP nodes in a single second.

The Laws of Physics (Real World)

However, these 1,000,000 virtual nodes possess no physical silicon chips and consume no actual electricity; thus, their collective hashrate share is effectively zero.

In a PoW environment, without the expenditure of real physical energy, a Sybil node cannot produce a valid block. Trust is anchored in physics.

Section 5: Solutions
The Longest Chain Rule & Game Theory
Phase Six: The Ultimate Lie of a Byzantine Traitor (Double Spending)

A malicious organization attempts to default on a past transaction. After receiving a service, they secretly mine a hidden, alternative branch (Chain B) in an effort to rewrite history and claw back their spent funds.

Phase Seven: The Satoshi Consensus (The Longest Chain Rule)

All honest nodes in the network are hardcoded to only recognize the blockchain that has accumulated the most Proof-of-Work (the longest chain) as the sole historical truth.

Phase Eight: Hashrate Race and Economic Game Theory

For the traitor's fraudulent Chain B to be accepted by the network, their block generation speed must outpace and overtake the combined power of all other honest nodes (a 51% Attack).

The Economic Defense Line: Attempting to capture 51% of global computational power requires an astronomical investment in hardware and electricity. If an attacker mounts such an onslaught and destroys system trust, the underlying system value plummets to zero, causing the attacker's massive capital investment to go completely up in smoke. Honesty is the only viable strategy for profit maximization.

Section 5: Solutions
The "Absolute Truth Chain" (SSOT)
Shifting the Paradigm from Cryptocurrency to Enterprise Architecture

Gogoro and Taishin Bank abandon isolated databases and fragile real-time API synchronizations. Instead, they log all cross-organizational transaction data, point clearings, and account deductions directly into a decentralized shared ledger secured by PoW consensus.

The Root of Traditional Reconciliation

Historically, reconciliation exists because both parties maintain separate, disconnected books (double ledgers), leaving room for data discrepancies, human error, or data manipulation.

The PoW Ledger Disruption

Guarded by a computational moat and work proofs, data becomes completely irreversible once committed to the blockchain.

Consequently, the distinction between "Gogoro's data" and "Taishin Bank's data" vanishes. The network maintains only one unified "Absolute Truth Chain".
